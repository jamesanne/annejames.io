What museums get wrong about AI-generated wall text

A quick checklist for catching confidently-wrong descriptions before a visitor does.

A visitor reading a museum label assumes a curator stands behind every word. When a generative tool drafts that label, the assumption stays and the accountability quietly disappears, unless someone puts it back.

This post walks through the three failure modes I see most often, and a short checklist for catching them before a visitor does.

1. Confident, plausible, wrong

Language models do not hedge the way scholars do. A date, a maker or a material can be stated with total confidence and be entirely invented, and the sentence around it will read beautifully. The more polished the text, the less likely anyone is to check it.

2. Descriptions that flatten context

A label for a ceremonial object is not only a description. It carries decisions about who is credited, which terms are used and what is left unsaid. Those decisions often follow agreements with source communities that a model has never seen.

A label is a promise from the institution. Nobody signed this one.

3. Nobody owns the correction

Even good processes fail when a wrong label is spotted and nobody knows who is allowed to change it. Decide the owner and the turnaround before the first machine-drafted label goes on the wall.

Before you publish a machine-drafted label

  • A named person has checked every date, name and material against the record.
  • Sensitive or sacred items are reviewed with the relevant community, not just the curator.
  • Visitors can see that AI assisted, and know how to report an error.
  • Someone owns corrections, with a target turnaround.

Where to start

Pick one gallery and one label type, run the checklist for a month, and note what it catches. That small pilot will tell you more about your real risks than any policy written in advance.
